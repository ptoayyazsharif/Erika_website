Start here

First: they are not your down payment.

This is the one that trips up almost everybody, so I start here every single time.

Your down payment is your money going into the house. It becomes your equity the moment the deed is recorded. Closing costs are the fees required to legally transfer ownership and complete the transaction. Different pile, different purpose, budgeted separately.

You need both. If you have saved a down payment and nothing else, you have saved for part of the purchase, not for the purchase.

“It’s not the cost, it’s the surprise — and most surprises are preventable.”

Closing costs are the most misunderstood number in a home purchase. Not because they are complicated — they really aren’t — but because most people meet them for the first time on a settlement statement three days before they are supposed to sign.

That is the whole problem, and it is a fixable one. Everything in this guide is knowable weeks before you get to the table. Read it once now, while nothing is on the line, and the closing statement stops being a shock and starts being a checklist.

“The closing table is not the place for first-time questions. Preparation is what creates calm.”

Every figure in this guide is dated and sourced. Fees change, tax rules change, and your own numbers depend on your lender, your price and what you negotiate. Use this to know what to ask — then get your real numbers in writing.

Closing Costs 10102

The anatomy

The four buckets every fee falls into.

Every line on that settlement statement belongs to one of four groups. Once you can sort them, the page stops being intimidating.

Bucket one

Loan-related fees

Origination, underwriting, the credit report, the appraisal, and discount points if you buy your rate down. This is what it costs to have someone lend you money.

Bucket two

Title & legal

The title search, the closing attorney and title insurance. In Georgia a licensed attorney conducts your closing — that is not optional here, and it is not a fee you shop away.

Bucket three

Government fees

Recording the deed, the Georgia transfer tax and the intangible recording tax on your loan. Small words, real dollars. Page 05 has the exact rates.

Bucket four

Prepaid items

Your first year of homeowners insurance, the property tax escrow, and interest for the days between closing and your first payment.

Prepaids are not fees.

They are funds set aside for future bills — your insurance, your taxes, your interest. Nobody is keeping that money. You were always going to pay it. It is simply being collected early so the house is never uninsured and the taxes are never late. This is where people feel cheated, and they shouldn’t.

Then, separately, there are the real estate commissions, which are set in your contract and your brokerage agreement rather than by anybody’s fee schedule.

Closing Costs 10103

The split

So who pays what?

Broadly: buyers carry most of the closing costs, but sellers are not off the hook.

If you’re buying

2–5%of the purchase price

  • Loan fees — origination, underwriting, appraisal
  • Title costs and the closing attorney
  • Prepaids — insurance, tax escrow, interest
  • The intangible recording tax on your loan

If you’re selling

0.5–1.5%plus commissions

  • Commissions — much the largest single piece
  • The Georgia transfer tax, customarily
  • Any credit you agree to give the buyer
  • Payoff and lien-release costs on your own loan

“It is different and negotiable every single time.”

Just because someone paid for something the last time you bought a house does not mean they will pay for it this time. Seller-paid closing costs, a rate buydown funded by the seller, a credit in place of a repair — all of that is written, not assumed. Which is exactly why who is representing you matters.

Why your number won’t match your neighbour’s

Two buyers can go under contract on the identical house in the identical week and walk into two genuinely different sets of closing costs. That is normal. It is not a sign that one of them is being taken advantage of.

What moves the number: your loan type (conventional, FHA, VA and USDA all price differently), the purchase price, the location, which lender you chose, which title company and attorney are handling it, and whatever the two sides negotiated.

Georgia specifics

The two taxes nobody warns you about.

Two of those government line items are specific to this state, and they are worth knowing before you see them on a statement.

Closing Costs 10105

Two things people conflate

Earnest money, and title insurance.

Earnest money is not a closing cost

It is the deposit you put down to show the seller you are serious. In Georgia it is often around 1% of the purchase price, though there is no rule that fixes it. It is not an extra cost. It is your money, held, and then credited back to you at closing.

The arithmetic, because it lands better than the explanation

You’re buying a home at$300,000
You put down earnest money of$3,000
At closing you’re told you owe$5,000
So what you actually bring$2,000

Not $8,000. Your $3,000 is already sitting there working for you. And as long as you hold up your end of the contract and stay inside your deadlines, it is refundable. Those deadlines are the part people miss.

Title insurance — the Carfax for your house

When you buy a home you are not just buying the structure. You are buying the right to own it without someone appearing later to say actually, this is mine. Title insurance protects you from problems tied to the property’s past: old liens, unpaid taxes, clerical errors, forged documents, unknown heirs.

  • It is a one-time cost paid at closing that protects you for as long as you own the home. This is not homeowners insurance. You do not renew it every year.
  • Lender’s title insurance protects the bank — period. If a problem surfaces, the lender gets made whole, not you.
  • Owner’s title insurance is the one that protects you, and it is usually optional. Most buyers never learn they had a choice until it is too late to make one.

“Real estate isn’t expensive, but ignorance certainly is.”

Ask before closing day, not on it

  • Which of these lines can I shop for, and which are fixed?
  • Is the owner’s title policy included in this estimate — and if not, what does it cost?
  • How much of my total is prepaids rather than fees?
  • What would change if I put a different amount down?
  • What is the last day this estimate holds, and what would move it?

Keep a buffer on top of this number. Not because something has gone wrong, but because real estate involves timing, operations and a lot of moving pieces. A cushion is not pessimism, it is planning.
