First, your Closing Disclosure. It's a five-page form with the final details of your loan. Your loan terms, your projected monthly payments, and what you'll pay in fees and other costs to get the mortgage.

And the law says your lender has to make sure you receive it no later than three business days before you close.

So use those three days. Put it right next to your most recent Loan Estimate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's closing checklist points you to the table at the top of page three, because by law, only certain costs can change.

And ask your loan officer. Are the loan type, the interest rate, the monthly payment, the same as I expected? Are there fees I don't understand, or fees that changed a lot? Do I have an escrow account... and do I understand how it works?

Now, what if something changes after that? You get a corrected disclosure. But only three kinds of change start a brand new three-day wait. The A P R becomes inaccurate. The loan product changes. Or a prepayment penalty gets added. Other changes, you get the corrected form at or before closing, without a new wait.

Next, the final walkthrough.

The C F P B puts it simply. Before you sign any papers, do a final walk-through of the home.

Make sure everything you and the seller agreed would be repaired, has been repaired. And anything the seller agreed to leave in the house is still there. If it isn't, contact the seller right away to talk about it.

And things can change a little in those last few days. If the seller hasn't fixed something they agreed to fix, they might give you money toward your closing costs instead. That's called a seller credit.

Now here's the important part. Your money.

Your down payment and your closing costs are a tempting target. And here's how the scam works. Someone gets into the email of a real estate agent or a settlement agent. They watch for a closing.
