Let me explain.

First, how we got here. On January twentieth, twenty twenty-six, the President signed an executive order called "Stopping Wall Street From Competing With Main Street Homebuyers." It said large institutional investors should not buy single-family homes. And it asked for a law to make that stick.

And Congress delivered. The bill is called the twenty-first Century ROAD to Housing Act. It passed the Senate eighty-five to five. It passed the House three hundred fifty-eight to thirty-two. And it became law on July eleventh, twenty twenty-six.

The section we're talking about today is titled "Homes are for people, not corporations."

So what does it actually say? The heart of it is one sentence. No large institutional investor may purchase, or enter into a contract to purchase, any single-family home.

Now, who counts as a large institutional investor? It's a for-profit company or fund that, on its own or together with others, controls three hundred fifty or more single-family homes. Government agencies don't count. And a single-family home here means a building with two units or fewer. Manufactured homes aren't included.

When does it start? A hundred and eighty days after it became law. That's January seventh, twenty twenty-seven. And it runs for fifteen years.

And it has teeth. The penalty for breaking it can be up to a million dollars per violation, or three times the purchase price of the house, whichever is greater.

Now here's the important part. Everybody's calling it a ban. And for buying existing homes, it mostly is. But before you decide what it means for you, you need to know what it doesn't do.

Nobody has to sell. The law says, in plain words, that it doesn't require a large investor to sell any home it already owned. So the homes they own today... stay theirs.

They can still build. Build-to-rent communities, meaning homes built brand new to be rented out, are an exception. So are renovate-to-rent programs, where an investor fixes up a home that doesn't meet code, and puts at least fifteen percent of the purchase price into the work.

Rent-to-own programs can keep going too, if they report the renter's on-time rent to the credit bureaus, when the renter opts in. And some of those programs have to give the renter a right of first refusal and a thirty-day first look.

There are other exceptions as well. Foreclosures. Communities for people fifty-five and older. And for the first two years after the law starts, big investors can still buy from smaller investors.

And if you're a small landlord with a few rentals? You're nowhere near three hundred fifty homes. This law isn't about you.

So why should Atlanta care more than most places? Here's why.

Nationally, the big investors are a small slice. About three percent of single-family rentals, and less than half a percent of all single-family homes.
